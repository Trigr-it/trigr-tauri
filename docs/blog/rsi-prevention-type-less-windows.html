RSI Prevention for Windows Users: Type Less, Not Just Better

By The Keyfire team 5 October 2026 7 min read

Most advice on repetitive strain injury is about how you type. Wrist angle, chair height, the keyboard with the gentler switches. All of it worth doing. None of it changes the other half of the equation, which is how much you type and click in the first place.

The NHS describes RSI as pain caused by repeated movement and overuse, and lists typing and mouse work among the usual culprits. If repetition is the ingredient, every keystroke and click you avoid is prevention, and a surprising share of a desk worker's input was never needed at all. This article is about handing that input to software, using a Windows tool we build called Keyfire: macros, hotkeys, text expansion and a clipboard history, with no scripting.

Before anything else: this is a work-habits article, not medical advice. If you already have pain that persists overnight, tingling, numbness, or weakness in the hand or arm, speak to a GP or a physiotherapist first.

Where a workday's repetition actually goes

Watch a normal Windows desk day with a stopwatch and the repetition sorts itself into six buckets. The mix depends on the job, but the buckets are the same for an engineer, a paralegal, a support agent and a recruiter.

  • Fixed sequences. The same five or six inputs in the same order inside one application. Select all, copy, switch window, paste, confirm. You run it so often you no longer notice.
  • Awkward chords. Three- and four-key shortcuts that splay the hand: little finger anchored on Ctrl, thumb on Alt, a stretch for a letter on the far side.
  • Mouse trips. Reaching across, travelling to a menu, clicking through a folder tree four levels deep, coming back. The forearm rotates on every trip.
  • Boilerplate. The signature, the address, the standard reply you send a dozen times a week.
  • Corrections. Every typo costs at least two extra presses, and the same dozen words get mistyped the same way for years.
  • Re-copying. Copying something again because the clipboard only holds one thing.

None of these is dramatic on its own. The accumulation is the problem, and the accumulation is what a good ergonomic setup leaves untouched. A split keyboard makes each press cheaper. It does nothing about the count.

Cheaper keystrokes first, then fewer

Workstation setup is well covered elsewhere. In the UK, the Health and Safety Executive publishes free guidance on display screen equipment with a workstation checklist, and employers must assess the workstations of staff who use screens for much of the day. The points that matter most for hands: wrists level with the forearms, a mouse that does not force the palm flat, a screen that does not pull the shoulders forward. Do that once, properly. Then move on to the part most guides skip.

Six ways to delete input from your day

1. Fold a fixed sequence into one key

If you run the same sequence of keys and clicks more than a few times a day, it is a macro waiting to be written, and macros are where the biggest cuts live. A Keyfire macro is a list of steps picked from a menu: press this key, type this text, click here, wait a moment, focus that window. One press runs the whole list, with nothing to script. Our plain-English macro guide covers the basics. Three shapes come up again and again:

  • The transfer. Select all, copy, switch to the other app, paste, confirm. Nine presses by hand, run thirty times on a bad day. As a macro it is five steps and one key.
  • The form. Click the first field, type a code, Tab, type the next value, Enter. Type Text and Press Key steps rebuild it exactly, and a Text Expansion step with a fill-in field can pause for the one value that changes.
  • The batch. The same six presses repeated forty times down a list. Build it once, set the Loop to a count of forty, and take your hands off the desk while it runs. Esc stops it at any point.

If a sequence is easier to perform than to describe, record it. Keyfire's macro recorder captures your keys, clicks and mouse movement with their timing and replays them from one key, and a recording can be distilled into editable steps later. One detail that matters for RSI: build macros you tap to run, not ones you hold. A trigger you must keep pressed swaps repetition for a sustained grip, which is its own strain pattern. Keyfire fires on press, and a double tap of one key can carry a second action with no chord at all.

2. Replace the chord with a tap

The useful shortcuts in most applications are the three- and four-key chords that stretch the hand the most. Keep the shortcut and change what you press to get it: bind a single key you rarely use, an F-key say, to send the chord for you. One tap, hand relaxed, the application receives exactly what it expects. Most people have a handful of chords they fire fifty times a day, and each one moved to a tap removes the presses and the stretch together. For keys you would otherwise hold down, such as a nudge arrow in a drawing tool, Repeat Mode turns the key into a toggle: press to start repeating, press again to stop, nothing gripped in between.

3. Replace the mouse trip with a key

This is the bucket people underestimate. Typing uses the fingers. Mousing uses the whole forearm, usually rotated, usually reaching. Three routes keep the hands where they already are:

  • A hotkey for the destination. The folder four levels deep, the web app you open twenty times a day. Bind each to a key and the clicking is gone. See our custom hotkeys guide.
  • Quick Search. Press Ctrl+Space, type a few letters, press Enter. Every hotkey, expansion and macro you own is reachable without remembering its key.
  • The side buttons on the mouse. Keyfire binds the two thumb buttons like any key, including to a full macro. For more than two, a radial menu opens a wheel of your most-used actions under the cursor from one hotkey.

4. Stop retyping paragraphs you have already written

Text expansion is the largest raw keystroke saver for anyone who writes for a living. Type a short trigger such as ;sig and the full block appears. A 400-character signature typed five times a day is 2,000 presses; with an expansion it is four. Replies that are never quite identical still work: fill-in fields pause for the client's name and the deadline, and date tokens drop in today's date or the date three working days out. Our text expansion guide walks through it.

5. Let the software take the backspaces

Corrections are the most invisible repetition because each one is tiny. Office autocorrect stops at the edge of the Office window. Keyfire's autocorrect (a Pro feature) works in every application, including the browser, the ticketing system and the chat client where most typing happens. Add the words you personally get wrong and the fix happens as you type.

6. Stop copying the same thing twice

The Windows clipboard holds one item, so needing the thing you copied before the thing you just copied means a mouse trip, a selection and a keystroke. In Keyfire, Ctrl+Shift+V opens a searchable history of everything copied in the last seven days (longer on Pro) and pastes the one you pick. Pinned items stay at the top for the reference strings you reach for all week. We compared the options in our clipboard manager comparison.

The habits software cannot do for you

Pacing sits outside any tool. Tissues recover between loads, so short frequent pauses beat the occasional long one: twenty seconds with the hands off the desk every half hour, a proper stand and stretch every hour. Keyfire has no break reminder, so use whichever timer you will actually obey.

So does listening. RSI arrives gradually, which makes it easy to explain away. Tired hands after an unusually long day that feel normal by morning: keep going. Aching or stiffness on ordinary days that eases overnight and returns the next day: the cheap stage to act on, so tighten the breaks and move more sequences to macros now. Symptoms that last into the evening, numbness, weakness, dropping things: stop self-managing and book a GP or a physiotherapist.

A first afternoon, not a programme

  1. Ten minutes: install Keyfire, pick the sequence you ran most today, and either build it from steps or hit Record Macro, run it once by hand, and stop. Put it on a key you can reach without a chord.
  2. Five minutes: move the one three- or four-key shortcut you fire all day to a single tap, and bind hotkeys for the two or three folders and apps you reach for most.
  3. Ten minutes: add your signature, address and two most-sent replies as expansions, with a fill-in field for whatever changes.
  4. Five minutes: bind the mouse side buttons to the two actions you mouse to most, and pin the reference strings you paste every day in the clipboard history.
  5. Next week: check the analytics panel. Whatever still feels like repetition is next, and it is usually another macro.

Posture advice makes each keystroke cost less. This is the other lever, the one most guides never pull. Pull both.
